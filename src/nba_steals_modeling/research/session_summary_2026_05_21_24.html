Background: Why Steals?

After the points modeling post-mortem concluded the points market is too efficient to beat with rolling stats, we evaluated which other markets might have structural inefficiency. The shortlist:

  • Assists — role/pace sensitive, books lag on ball-handler changes
  • Steals — high variance, low-frequency stat, market may be lazy on 1.5 lines
  • Blocks — matchup-sensitive, thin market

We picked steals first because the 1.5 line is close to 50/50 and the data was ready (3 seasons in S3). The key hypothesis: books may anchor the 1.5-line over price to historical steal averages without adjusting for role/minutes, creating a persistent under edge.

⚠ Red Flags — Investigate Before Trusting

1. Odds tier breakdown contradicts overall ROI. All three odds tiers (-300 to -200, -200 to -150, -150 to -100) showed negative ROI (-1.2% to -1.6%). But overall ROI is +7.5%. Arithmetically impossible unless 666 out-of-bin rows are driving the whole number. These rows likely have under_odds outside [-300, -100] — possibly positive odds or extreme values from data errors.
2. Player-level ROIs are implausibly high.
  • Jalen Suggs: 50.8% hit rate, ROI +143% (impossible at -190 odds)
  • Jordan Poole: 65.5% hit rate, ROI +120%
  • Kawhi Leonard: 52.1% hit rate, ROI +99%
A 65% hit rate at -190 odds is roughly break-even. These numbers require wildly favorable odds — suggesting swapped over/under fields or data entry errors in those rows.
Audit required: Run df["under_odds"].describe() and plot the distribution. Flag rows where under_odds > -100 (positive odds on a typical -190 market) and rows where under_odds < -400. Remove those rows and rerun. If ROI survives, the signal is real.
